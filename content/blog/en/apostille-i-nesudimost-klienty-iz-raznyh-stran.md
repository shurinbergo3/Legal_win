# Apostille and Criminal Record Certificates for Clients from Many Countries

When you look at what people come to us with most often, two documents stay near the top of the list - the apostille and the certificate of no criminal record (zaświadczenie o niekaralności, an extract from the KRK). And the people who bring them are of very different origins: Ukrainians, Belarusians, Turkish citizens, Russians, sometimes Americans and EU citizens. The reason is simple - both documents are almost always needed not "here and now in Poland" but to cross a border: so a Polish document is accepted abroad, or a foreign one works in Poland. And cross-border legalisation is exactly where weeks can be lost for no good reason.

## Why these two documents in particular - the short answer

The apostille and the KRK are the most "border-facing" documents. They are rarely made for an internal Polish procedure and almost always to cross a jurisdiction: emigration, a visa, work abroad, a marriage in another country, permanent residence in Poland with documents from one's homeland. Here different bodies, different validity terms and the rigid "apostille first, translation second" order all meet. That is why people from the most varied countries come to us - their task is the same.

## Typical scenarios - by country

Over the years a recognisable picture forms. Below are generalised, anonymised scenarios - no real names, only the essence.

| Where the client is from | What is needed | Where to |
|---|---|---|
| Ukraine | Polish KRK + apostille | PR in Canada, express entry |
| Turkey | apostille on a marriage certificate | recognition of the marriage in Turkey |
| Belarus | home-country certificate + apostille | karta stałego pobytu in Poland |
| USA (expat in Poland) | Polish KRK + apostille | work permit in the UAE |
| Russia | apostille on a diploma and KRK | work and immigration abroad |
| Georgia / Kazakhstan | home-country certificate + translation | a permanent-residence package |
| EU citizen | USC odpis + multilingual form | a procedure in another EU country |

A pattern is visible: some carry Polish documents abroad, others carry documents from home into Poland. The legalisation mechanics are similar in both cases, but the direction and the set of bodies differ.

## What makes cross-border legalisation hard

At first glance the apostille looks like "one stamp". In practice there are several pitfalls, and they are exactly why people come for help.

- **The apostille is affixed by the issuing country.** The Polish MSZ will not legalise a Ukrainian or Kazakh document - its apostille is affixed at home. And vice versa.
- **Different bodies within Poland.** On the KRK the apostille is affixed by the MSZ, on USC certificates by the wojewoda, on a diploma by NAWA. Going to the "wrong" one means losing a day.
- **The EU exception.** Within the Union, under Regulation 2016/1191, the apostille is often unnecessary - but not everyone knows that and they pay for nothing.
- **The order of steps.** The apostille comes before the translation, otherwise the stamp text is left untranslated.
- **Validity windows.** The KRK and many certificates "live" for 3-6 months. Drag your feet and the document ages by the time of filing.

Each point on its own is not hard. The problem is that in a real case they come together, across several countries, and against a deadline.

## How we do it remotely

Most clients do not need to be present in person. We work on a power of attorney (a pełnomocnictwo, drawn up before a consul where needed), and that closes almost the whole process remotely:

1. We clarify the purpose and the destination country - it decides whether an apostille is needed at all and which body affixes it.
2. We obtain a fresh document - the KRK via e-KRK or on paper, an odpis from the USC, a home-country certificate through a consulate or partner.
3. We affix the apostille at the right body and do the sworn translation in the right order.
4. We hand over the finished package - in Poland or sent to the country where it is needed.

For the client this is usually one conversation and a power of attorney, not a series of visits to different offices and countries.

## A few anonymised stories

**Case A., Ukraine.** Preparing to immigrate to Canada, he needed a Polish KRK with apostille and translation. We made a fresh certificate, affixed the apostille at the MSZ and translated it - in the right order, with no re-translation of the stamp. We fit inside the validity window for filing.

**Case G., Turkey.** Married in Poland, he needed the document to have the marriage recognised at home. We apostilled the marriage certificate at the wojewoda (not the MSZ - a common mistake), then a sworn translation. One package, no trips between offices.

**Case N., Belarus.** Filing for the karta stałego pobytu, the wojewoda demanded a certificate of no criminal record from the country of origin with an apostille. We launched the home-country certificate first (the longest element), assembled the Polish part in parallel, and aligned the freshness of both certificates to the filing date.

Names and details are changed - but the logic in every case is the same: the right body, the right order, the right timing.

## When you can realistically manage on your own, and when to delegate

Honestly: one simple document to one country, many people arrange themselves. If you have a profil zaufany, the destination is within the EU, or the procedure is transparent - go ahead, that is a normal path.

It is worth delegating when the task stops being "one stamp":

- several documents for one package (immigration, a family visa);
- documents from a country where cooperation is difficult (Russia in 2024-2026);
- a hard deadline where you cannot get the body or the order wrong;
- you need to align the validity of the certificate, the apostille and the translation;
- you are abroad and personal visits to Polish offices are impossible.

In these cases the time saved and the absence of redos usually outweigh the fee.

## In short

The apostille and the certificate of no criminal record are documents where clients from the most varied countries converge, because both tasks are essentially about crossing a border. Technically everything is solvable, but the details - which body, what order, what timing - decide whether the document passes the first time or comes back. We are precisely about making it pass the first time.
